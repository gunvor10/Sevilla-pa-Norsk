Spain’s liquid gold

About the visit

Spain produces more olive oil than any other country in the world, and almost all of it comes from Andalucía. Drive out of Seville and it is not long before the landscape turns into rows of olive trees as far as you can see. But few people ever get to see what actually happens to the olives between the tree and the bottle.

That is what this visit is about. The estate is one of the oldest in the region, and it has both: the old mill with its stone wheels and the 18th-century beam press, and the modern press where the oil is made today. You follow the process from beginning to end, and learn why the differences in quality are so large.

The mill with its stone wheels, and the beam press that took several men to turn

Then there is the olive museum, which is the estate's real curiosity: over a hundred different olive trees from fourteen countries, planted side by side. Here you understand why the oil tastes so different from place to place — the varieties differ as much as wine grapes do. The peacocks wandering loose between the trees have nothing to do with olive oil, but they belong there all the same.

The carriage museum comes as a surprise to most people: a private collection of carriages, harnesses and traps, lined up in a long whitewashed hall. And the patios around the main house, with bougainvillea covering the whole wall, are the Andalucía people have seen in photographs and wonder whether it really exists.

Over a hundred olive trees from fourteen countries, each with its own name plate

And finally the tasting: four extra virgin oils in blue glasses, which hide the colour so you judge on aroma and taste alone. You learn the difference between the varieties, what makes an oil good, and which one suits what in the kitchen. Most people discover they have never really tasted olive oil before.

How the day runs

Start
Out of the city
The estate is half an hour's drive from the centre. I can arrange private return transport, or you can drive yourselves — whichever you prefer.
Part 1
The mill and the press
The old mill first: the stone wheels, the beam press, the clay jars where the oil was stored. Then the modern press, where you see how it is done today and why the differences in quality are so large.
Part 2
The museums and the patios
The olive museum with its hundred varieties from fourteen countries, the carriage museum with the horse-drawn carriages, and the patios around the main house. Bring a camera.
Part 3
The tasting
Four extra virgin oils in blue glasses, presented by the estate itself. You learn what to smell for, what bitterness and pungency mean, and which oil suits what.
Finish
Breakfast, picnic or home
If you chose the breakfast, that came first. If you chose the picnic, the table is laid among the trees now. Otherwise we drive back to the city with a bottle of oil in the luggage.
